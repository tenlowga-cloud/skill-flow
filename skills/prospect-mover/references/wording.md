# Wording: the systems under every word

Most decisions run on fast, automatic thinking (Kahneman's System 1). It judges by ease, feeling and familiarity before slow thinking (System 2) checks anything. Words that are easy to process feel truer, safer and more likable. That is the whole game at the word level: make the right idea effortless to take in.

## 1. Processing fluency: easy reads as true

- Short, common words. "Use" not "utilize". "Help" not "facilitate". "Cost" not "investment" unless the reader uses that word.
- One idea per sentence. Most sentences under 15 words. Vary the rhythm: a short punch after a longer line.
- Concrete beats abstract. "Your phone stops ringing at 6pm" beats "better work-life balance". "Dry basement by Friday" beats "waterproofing solutions". People can picture the first.
- Cut every word that doesn't change meaning. Read it aloud. Where you stumble, they stop.
- Consumer copy lands around grade 5 to 7. Experts still prefer simple: plain writing reads as more intelligent, not less.

## 2. Framing: the same fact, different decisions

- **Loss aversion.** A loss hurts roughly twice as much as an equal gain pleases (Kahneman and Tversky). "You're losing about $3,000 a month in missed calls" moves more than "capture $3,000 more a month". Use it honestly: name a real cost of inaction, never invent a threat.
- **Attribute framing.** "9 out of 10 customers renew" lands better than "1 in 10 cancel". Pick the frame that is true and points toward the action.
- **Gain frame for prevention and health-positive actions, loss frame for detection and protection gaps.** When unsure, pair them: the loss of standing still, then the gain of acting.
- **Price framing.** Shrink to the smallest honest unit ("$1.40 a day") and compare to a familiar spend. Bundle the pain (one price), unbundle the value (list what they get).
- **Ownership language.** "Your plan", "your quote", "your family's number". People value what feels theirs (endowment effect).

## 3. Specificity: exact feels true

- Exact numbers beat round ones. "$412" beats "about $400". "17 minutes" beats "a few minutes". Only use numbers you can source.
- Named beats general. "A retired teacher in Lakewood" (true, and with permission) beats "a client". "Tuesday at 2" beats "sometime this week".
- Sensory and situational detail makes a scene. "The kitchen table at 11pm with the bills spread out" beats "financial stress".

## 4. You focus: they are the hero

- Count "you/your" against "we/our/I". Aim for 2 to 1 or better. Rewrite sentences that start with "We".
- Lead with their situation, not your credentials. Credentials go in the proof beat.
- Use their words. Pull language from call notes, reviews, forms and objections. The prospect's own phrasing is the most persuasive copy that exists.

## 5. Reasons: the word "because"

Giving a reason, even a simple one, raises compliance sharply (Langer's copier study). Every ask gets a real reason: "Book the inspection this month because the warranty window closes in June." The reason must be true.

## 6. Questions over claims

- A claim invites a counterargument. A question invites the reader to persuade themselves.
- "If your best tech quit tomorrow, who would answer the after-hours calls?" does more than "Our answering service is reliable".
- One question per short piece. Never stacked. Never obvious ("Do you love your family?").

## 7. Labels and calm language (Voss)

- **Labels** name the feeling: "It sounds like you've been burned by an agent before." It lowers defenses and shows you get it.
- **Accusation audit**: say the bad thing they're thinking first. "You're probably expecting a sales pitch. This isn't one."
- **No-oriented questions** feel safer than yes questions: "Would it be a bad idea to look at this before your renewal?" "Are you against a 10-minute call?"

## 8. Power words, used sparingly

Words that consistently pull attention: you, free (only if truly free), new, because, now (with a real reason), instantly, easy, proven (with proof), your name. Overuse kills them. One or two per piece.

## 9. Kill list

These leak trust or energy. Remove on sight:

- Openers: "I hope this finds you well", "My name is", "I wanted to reach out", "Just checking in", "Quick question" (followed by a long one).
- Hedges: "just", "kind of", "maybe", "I think", "possibly", "a bit", "hopefully".
- Empty superlatives: "best-in-class", "world-class", "industry-leading", "cutting-edge", "solutions", "one-stop shop".
- Pressure without a reason: "act now", "limited time", "don't miss out", "last chance" (unless true and stated why).
- Risk filler: "no pressure", "no obligation", "we're not here to sell". They flag the sale. Remove the risk another way: make the step small and specific, or say what happens if everything is fine.
- AI tells and em dashes (see `ghost-mode`).

## 10. Rhythm and endings

- Serial position: people remember the first and last things best. Put the hook first and the ask last.
- End on the action or the strongest image, never on a disclaimer sentence you could move up, a "let me know if you have any questions", or a sign-off paragraph.

## 11. House style for one-to-one notes

Good general rules for any email or text a person sends in their own name. The owner's `VOICE.md` overrides them where it differs.

- **A plain personal note, not a brochure.** It reads like a note from one real person who signs it. No salesy design, no product names in early touches. Sell the outcome.
- **One concrete situation, one natural question.** Raise one real situation and end the body with one question the person can answer.
- **End on a question, not a reply instruction.** Not "Reply YES" and not "Reply HELP and my team will reach out". A question gets a human answer.
- **Book with either/or choices.** "Morning or afternoon better this week?" beats "let me know when you're free".
- **Never a hard question cold.** Set it up first ("this is all preliminary") or reframe before a sensitive one. A recent, specific, real story beats a statistic. Use plain comparisons for hard concepts.
- **Don't assume their life.** "How would the bills get paid?" not "the house payment". Don't assume they are working, married, a homeowner or a business owner unless you know.
- **Breakup lines say what is ending.** "This is the last time I'll bring this up. If you ever want me to look at what you've got, I'm here." beats "Should I close this?".
- **A short nudge works with warm contacts.** A forwarded "Did you get this?" is fine when they already know you.
- **Sign as a person.** A named sender, not "The team at". Sign-off and banned words come from `VOICE.md`.
