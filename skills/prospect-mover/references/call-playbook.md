# Call playbook: live conversations in any vertical

How to move someone on a call, a video meeting, a voicemail or the follow-up after one. Patterns drawn from reviewing real recorded sales calls, stated so they work for any product or service. The examples rotate verticals on purpose. If the owner has a vertical lens (`lens-example.md` shows the shape), it adds the vertical's own call moves and compliance rewrites.

Evidence level: small sample. These are patterns to test against your own calls, not laws.

## The one finding that held on every call

**Every call that ended with a specific day and hour moved forward. Every call that ended on "think it over", "email me" or "let me know" stalled.** Book the next step live, as a time, before you hang up.

## Know the close before you dial

Write down the close (the final yes) and the rung this call has to win (`structure.md#the-close-ladder`). A discovery call wins the numbers meeting. A numbers call wins the signature. End the call on that rung, booked as a day and an hour.

## Moves that work

1. **Open on them, with permission.** "Did I catch you at an okay time?" then "What's got you busy today?" Note the time window they give you and honor it out loud: "You've got fifteen minutes, I'll take five."
2. **Service first, sale second.** When there's a real reason to talk (a renewal, a check-in, a fix, a question they asked), handle it fully. Then raise the gap as a question, not a claim. A roofer says "Did anyone check the flashing when they did the gutters?" A SaaS rep says "How are you handling approvals right now?"
3. **Get curious about what they're proud of.** "How did you build that?" "How do you do that in this market?" It reveals who they think they are (investor, builder, careful planner), and the pitch should mirror that identity back.
4. **Give before you ask.** One or two genuinely useful tips that help even if they never buy earn you the right to make the pitch.
5. **Run a commercial before sensitive questions.** "What I'll do today is look at your whole [setup, situation, operation] so it all works together. That means a few direct questions, so I can give you specific recommendations instead of generalities. Sound fair?" Wait for the yes. Without it, people get defensive and leave early.
6. **Ask about the problem, not the product.** "What made you buy the one you have? What problem were you solving? Is that still the problem?" Selling happens during discovery. By the end, they should have named the gap in their own words.
7. **Hard questions get facts, soft questions get the why.** Hard: "How many people use it?" Soft: "Silly question, but if that person left tomorrow, what would happen?"
8. **Assume they're competent.** "What are you using for this today?" not "Do you have anything for this?"
9. **Let them rule out the wrong option.** "It doesn't sound like you need the advanced tier. Is that accurate?" They say no, and the right option feels like their idea.
10. **Stakes first, then a small honest price.** Name what the gap costs, then the price in the smallest honest unit ("about a dollar a day", "less than one missed job a month"). Offer the payment option that matches how they think about money (annual, monthly, per project).
11. **Size the need with them before you quote.** Do a quick, transparent calculation together (hours lost a week, jobs missed a month, the gap in dollars). People guess low until they see their own numbers.
12. **Ask the budget in terms of the problem.** "If we solved the things we talked about, what would you be comfortable putting toward it?" Then state the real price plainly. Never decide for them what's expensive.
13. **Lead with good news when it's real.** "Your setup qualified for the best tier we offer. I've only seen that a handful of times." Only when it's true.
14. **Scale big anchors down to something they can picture,** then propose a small first step or a test amount.
15. **The humor veto.** "I'll put a couple of options together, and you can tell me, 'You're an idiot, I don't like this.'" It makes agreeing to look easy.
16. **Remove the risk with facts.** "It takes five minutes, and nothing gets charged until [the real condition]." A specific, true reason it's safe works. Vague filler ("no pressure") doesn't.
17. **Sweep with "anything else?" before you hang up.** "Any other questions, or anything else I should look at or consider?" It often surfaces the real objection while there's still time to answer it.
18. **Book it live, with two specific slots.** "Tuesday at nine, or one?" "Let's do the 29th at two. Sound good?" Then: "You'll get a reminder." Send the calendar invite while you're still on the call.
19. **Bring in the other decision maker.** "You and [partner, cofounder, boss] can look at it tonight." Pair that with a set time to talk next.
20. **Refer out when it's not yours.** "That's not my lane, but I know someone good." It builds trust and earns referrals.

## Leaks that cost deals

1. **Pitching before finding a gap.** Two pitches in a row, neither tied to anything they said, lost the call. Ask one question that connects the offer to their situation first: "If [the thing they're proud of] hit a bad month, what would you fall back on?"
2. **Leading with heavy stories.** Dark or fearful stories in the first minutes, especially to someone who feels secure, shut people down. Save your story for after they've named a gap, and keep it to one sentence.
3. **Ending on "So,".** Every explanation ends with a question or an ask. A trailing "Right, so..." hands them the exit.
4. **Accepting "just email me" with no time attached.** Turn it into a slot: "Sure, I'll send one page tonight. Thursday at 5:30 or Friday at noon to go over it?" Never leave the next move on them ("text me if you have questions"), and never warn that your own email might land in spam.
5. **Pitching with no size and no price.** "We're competitive, I'll send something over" leaves nothing to react to. Give one sized option with its real price, or don't pitch that offer on this call.
6. **Missing the buying signal.** When they start selling themselves ("should I do more?", "could it cover X too?"), stop explaining and ask for the number or the next step.
7. **Inconsistent numbers.** Two different prices for the same thing in one call breaks trust. Have the numbers written down before you dial.
8. **Unexplored "later".** "We're waiting until January" needs one question: "What are you waiting to see?" Then set a dated check-in.
9. **Too many offers at once.** Several add-ons in a short call blur them all. One gap per call, maybe two.

## Voicemail and follow-up

- **Voicemail:** under 25 seconds. Your name, one specific reason tied to them, an easy next step, and your number twice, slowly. Send a text so they can reply without calling back.
- **Follow-up after a call:** recap their situation in their words, give the recommendation and the number, say what happens next, and close with a question that sets a time. The writing rules in `wording.md#house-style-for-one-to-one-notes` apply.

## Outreach that books the call

- Hook on a real pain you can actually fix. Generic "want more X?" hooks get a yes from everyone and a reply from no one.
- Combine two pains when they're real: "How do I [keep doing the work I'm good at] and [handle the thing everyone says I need]?"
- The body is one or two paragraphs, and its only job is to book the call. The pitch happens live.
- A sequence hits a different pain in each touch.
- Test it by asking: would someone in their exact situation click this?
