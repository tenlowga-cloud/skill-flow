## The behavior equation

BJ Fogg: **Behavior = Motivation × Ability × Prompt**, all at the same moment. If someone isn't acting, one of the three is missing.

- **Low motivation:** sharpen the gap, add proof, tie to what they care about. Don't push harder.
- **Low ability:** this is the most common leak and the cheapest fix. Make the action smaller, shorter, simpler: one tap, one reply, one field. Cutting friction usually beats adding motivation.
- **No prompt:** a clear ask at the right moment, a reminder, a calendar link.

Diagnose which one is missing before rewriting.

## Cialdini's 7 principles

Robert Cialdini, *Influence* and *Pre-Suasion*. Each with the honest use and the abuse to avoid.

| Principle | Why it works | Honest use | Abuse (never) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reciprocity** | People repay what they're given | Give real value first: a free audit or check, a useful calculator, a sample, a clear answer | Gifts with hidden strings, guilt |
| **Commitment and consistency** | People act in line with what they've said or done | Start with a tiny yes (a reply, a quiz answer), then build. Remind them of their own stated goal | Trick questions, pre-checked opt-ins they didn't choose |
| **Social proof** | People follow people like them, most under uncertainty | Specific, similar, real: "Most shops our size start with the two-truck plan" (if true) | Fake reviews, invented counts, "1,000 people bought today" |
| **Authority** | People defer to credible experts | State the credential that matters to this buyer once, plainly. Show expertise through a precise insight | Borrowed titles, fake badges, white-coat theater |
| **Liking** | People say yes to people they like | Real similarity, genuine compliments, warmth (see `vibe.md`) | Faked rapport, invented common ground |
| **Scarcity** | People want what's rare or going away, and losses loom large | True limits only: a real enrollment window, a real rate change date, real capacity. Say why it's limited | Fake countdowns, invented "only 3 left", artificial deadlines |
| **Unity** | People favor "us" (family, place, tribe, identity) | Shared identity that's real: "Fellow Denver dads", "As a small-business owner myself", "Built by nurses, for nurses" | Claiming an identity you don't share |

**Pre-suasion:** what a person sees just before the message sets how they read it. Lead with the frame you want: open with safety before a protection offer, with a success story before a price, with their goal before your plan.

## Decision biases that matter most in sales

| Bias | What it means | How to use it honestly |
|---|---|---|
| **Status quo bias** | Doing nothing feels safe, even when it isn't | Make inaction visible and costly in true terms. Make switching feel small. The real competitor is "later". |
| **Loss aversion** | Losses hurt about twice as much as gains | Frame the true cost of the gap. Pair with a clear, easy fix so fear turns into action, not freezing. |
| **Anchoring** | The first number sets the scale | Show the honest reference point first (the full cost of the risk, the price of the bigger plan), then the recommended option. |
| **Contrast principle** | Things are judged against what came just before | Present the larger relevant option first, then the right-sized one. |
| **Decoy effect** | A third, clearly worse option makes one option look better | Three tiers with the recommended one in the middle and marked. Only real, sellable options. |
| **Default effect** | People keep the pre-selected option | Pre-select the choice that's genuinely in their interest. Make opting out easy. |
| **Optimism bias** | "It won't happen to me" | Use a specific, relatable scenario, never a scare. "Identifiable victim": one real story moves more than a statistic. |
| **Present bias** | Now beats later | Give an immediate win (the number today, peace of mind tonight). Shrink the future cost to today's terms. |
| **Ostrich effect** | People avoid info that might be bad | Make looking feel safe and quick: "A 10-minute check. If you're covered, I'll tell you." |
| **Choice overload** | Too many options, fewer decisions | Three options max, one recommended. |
| **Endowment effect** | People value what feels theirs | "Your plan", personalized quotes, a trial, a saved draft. |
| **IKEA effect** | People value what they helped build | Let them choose a piece (the term length, the start date). |
| **Foot in the door** | A small yes makes a bigger yes likelier | Ladder the asks: reply, then call, then quote, then app. |
| **Zeigarnik effect** | Unfinished things nag | Progress bars, "you're 2 steps from your quote", a cliffhanger subject line that pays off. |

## Choice architecture

Design the moment of decision:

1. Reduce options to three or fewer, recommend one.
2. Pre-select the good default.
3. Remove every step, field and click you can.
4. Put the risk-reducer next to the ask.
5. Show what happens next and how long it takes.
6. Time the prompt to a real trigger (after the aha moment, before a true deadline, at a life event).

## The abuse list

Never ship these, however well they'd convert:

- Fake scarcity or deadlines, countdown timers that reset.
- Fake social proof: invented reviews, counts, logos, "as seen on".
- Confirmshaming ("No thanks, I don't care about my family").
- Hidden costs, bait and switch, pre-checked add-ons.
- Fear beyond the facts, especially with older or grieving prospects.
- Borrowed authority or misleading credentials.
- Making cancellation or saying no hard.

They fail the daylight test, burn trust and referrals, and in regulated work can break the law.
