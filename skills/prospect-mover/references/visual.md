# Visual: color, layout, type and the button

People judge a page's credibility and appeal in a fraction of a second, mostly from how it looks. Attractive, clean design is also judged easier to use (the aesthetic-usability effect). Visual work decides whether the words get read at all.

## 1. Color: contrast and consistency beat hue

Honest caveat: color meanings are cultural and context dependent, and lab claims ("red sells more") don't transfer cleanly. What reliably works is **contrast, consistency and fit with the brand and the feeling**. Use the associations below as a starting point, not a law.

| Color | Common association | Good for | Watch out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blue, navy | Trust, calm, stability | Finance, health, B2B, software | Can read cold or generic. Add warmth elsewhere. |
| Green | Money, growth, go, health | Savings, the go button, wellness | Neon green reads cheap. |
| Deep navy or black with gold or warm white | Premium, serious, high end | Luxury, high-ticket services, premium tiers | Too much gold reads gaudy. |
| Warm neutrals (cream, sand, warm gray) | Approachable, human, calm | Family, retirement, local service | Low contrast if text is light. |
| Orange, coral | Energy, friendly action | Buttons, highlights | Overuse reads discount. |
| Red | Urgency, danger, stop | Errors, a true deadline | Wrong for money and risk topics as a main color. |

**The rules that do hold:**

- **Isolation effect (Von Restorff).** The one thing that looks different gets noticed and remembered. Use one accent color, and use it only on the one action. If everything is highlighted, nothing is.
- **60/30/10.** About 60% dominant neutral, 30% secondary brand color, 10% accent. The accent is the button.
- **Contrast for reading.** Body text at least 4.5:1 against its background (WCAG AA), larger headings at least 3:1. Light gray text on white loses older readers fast. Check who the buyer is.
- **Consistency.** The same colors, the same button style, everywhere the prospect touches you. Consistency reads as trustworthy.

## 2. Hierarchy: one thing first

- The eye needs one entry point: the headline or a face. Then the supporting line. Then the action.
- Size, weight and white space create order. Use at most three text sizes on a screen.
- People scan in F and Z patterns. Put the hook top left or center, the action where the scan ends.
- Squint test: blur your eyes. The headline and the button should still stand out. If they don't, the hierarchy is broken.

## 3. White space reads as premium

- Crowding reads cheap and anxious. Space reads confident and expensive.
- Cut elements before shrinking them. Less on the page, more of it read.
- Short paragraphs (1 to 3 lines on a phone), generous line height (about 1.5).

## 4. Type

- One or two typefaces. A clean sans for UI and body on screens. A serif or a distinct display face for headlines when you want premium or editorial warmth.
- Body text 16px minimum on mobile, 18px for older audiences.
- Sentence case headlines read friendlier than Title Case or ALL CAPS.

## 5. Imagery

- **Faces pull attention,** and people follow a face's gaze. Point the gaze at the headline or the button.
- **Real beats stock.** A real photo of the owner, the team or the work beats any stock handshake. Stock reads as "this could be anyone".
- **Show the after,** the relief or the outcome, not the product or the paperwork.
- Every image earns its place or goes.

## 6. The button (and any call to action)

- Label it with the outcome in first person: "Get my number", "See my quote", "Book my 15 minutes". Not "Submit", "Learn more", "Click here".
- One primary button per screen. Secondary actions look secondary (text link, outline).
- Big and easy to tap (at least 44 by 44 px, ideally full width on phones). Fitts' law: bigger and closer, faster and more clicks.
- A risk-reducer right under it, specific not filler: "Takes 2 minutes." "Written quote on the spot." "Cancel anytime in two clicks." Skip "no pressure" and "no obligation" filler (`wording.md#kill-list`).

## 7. Friction and forms

- Every field costs completions. Ask only what you need now. Name and phone beats a 12-field form. Ask the rest later.
- Break long forms into small steps with progress shown. Start with the easiest question.
- Defaults do the work: pre-select the most common choice when it's in their interest.

## 8. Phone first

Most prospects see it on a phone. Check every visual piece at 390px wide before calling it done (`pixel-proof`). The hook, the proof and the button should be reachable without hunting.
